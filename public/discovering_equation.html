Discovering process
The method I used to find the solution followed this pattern: - Start with no clear idea. - Make a random guess. - Identify why the guess is wrong. - Understand the mistake and refine the guess with better knowledge. ### First Steps The initial issue was that the traditional method violates Newton's Third Law. To fix this, I started by guessing a force that depends on the angle between the velocities. I then realized that velocity must be considered relative, meaning the force between two particles can only depend on their relative velocity \\(\Delta v\\). ### Refining the Approach Through logical reasoning and unit analysis, I concluded that the force should depend on \\(\Delta v^2\\) rather than just \\(\Delta v\\). However, no force equation I tested seemed to work. Eventually, I realized that ignoring the charge of protons was a mistake, protons also contribute to the force, just like electrons. ### A Key Breakthrough I performed a quick check to see if a force of the form $$F = \frac{\Delta v^2}{r^2}$$ flipped correctly when the electron's velocity was flipped, and it worked. (2 parallel wires, and we flip the currect of one of the wires) This gave me confidence that a better force equation existed. (this is around 1-2 years since the beginning). ### Exploring Charge and Resistance I then focused on understanding the speed + charges of electrons within wires, investigating how charge and velocity change with different resistances. Unfortunately, I wasted about a year on this approach. ### Switching to a Computational Approach Starting over, I wrote a Python program to manually integrate the force equations and began testing forces that would match the results of the traditional method. After another 1-2 years, I found a strange force equation: $$F = \frac{v^2 - v_r^2}{r^2}$$ However, it violated energy conservation. While experimenting with the code, I discovered another force term of the form \\(F \thickapprox a/r\\). This suggested something was off, so I discarded the approach. ### Shifting to Wire part as a "particle" Instead of analyzing individual electrons and protons separately, I started treating each wire segment as a whole one particle. This led me to a critical realization: Instead of working with force directly, I should focus on energy, since force can be derived from energy in a way that naturally preserves energy and momentum. I eventually found a perfect equation for wire segments, but it failed to account for changing currents. ### Speed Realization I then realized that when the wire moves, all that changes is the velocity, meaning force must depend on velocity. This led me back to analyzing protons and electrons separately. I discovered another force equation: $$F = \frac{\frac{1}{2}v_r^2 - v_{r_\bot}^2}{r^2}$$ which turned out to be mathematically equivalent to my previous result (just a simple Pythagorean relation). This hinted that I was converging on the only solution thats there. ### Energy Breakthrough This time, I approached the problem from an energy perspective. To avoid violating energy conservation, I realized that force must also depend on acceleration. From this, I finally arrived at the complete solution, which exactly describes the forces between wire segments (for freely moving particles advanced investigation is required).
